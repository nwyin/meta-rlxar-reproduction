Read the whole visible paper before writing anything. Your rubric must fit this paper and
this section, not papers or sections in general. A grader will score one candidate at a
time against it, so every criterion must be checkable by holding the candidate next to the
paper.

Build the criteria from the paper's own content. Identify what the paper claims, what
method or evidence it rests on, what results it reports, and what the surrounding sections
already say and assume. Then decide what this section type must do for this paper: an
abstract must state this paper's problem, approach, and main findings; an introduction
must motivate this problem and lead into this paper's contributions; a related work
section must position this paper against the specific lines of prior work it builds on or
departs from; a conclusion must sum up these results and their limits. Turn those demands
into criteria.

Name specifics in the criteria and anchors. Refer to the paper's actual contributions,
methods, datasets, results, terms, and cited work, so the grader can verify a candidate
against the paper rather than rely on general impressions.

Write anchors that describe observable features of a candidate at that level, not
adjectives. The low anchor should describe a plausible but inadequate candidate, the high
anchor one that could stand in the published paper. Keep criteria distinct so no two
reward the same thing.
