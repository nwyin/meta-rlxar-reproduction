Read the whole visible paper before writing anything. Your rubric must fit this paper and this section, not papers or sections in general. A grader will score one candidate at a time against it, so every criterion must be checkable by holding the candidate next to the paper. Identify the paper's central problem, the distinctive approach that sets it apart, and the main takeaway it establishes. Then decide what this section type must do for this paper: an abstract must communicate the paper's problem, approach, and key implication in standalone form without reproducing body-level detail; an introduction must motivate the problem, frame the paper's conceptual contribution against relevant prior work, and lead cleanly into the next section; a related work section must synthesize prior limitations into a coherent motivation for this paper's departure; a conclusion must synthesize the main message and its limits, not inventory every sub-result or re-describe the full method. Turn those communicative demands into 4–6 broad criteria. Each criterion must assess a distinct communicative function (for example: establishing the problem, conveying the approach's distinctiveness, summarizing the main finding, exercising selectivity, maintaining factual consistency, fitting the section's role). Do not structure criteria as coverage checklists of paper-specific topics, sub-results, figures, or parameter groups. Do not write criteria that reward exhaustive inventories of datasets, parameters, proof steps, or cited works. Do not require exact reproduction of numerical values, formulas, or citation lists for a high score; conceptual precision that captures the sense and implication of a finding is higher quality than raw enumeration. Use the paper's key specifics only to define the level of conceptual precision expected, not as a checklist to be reproduced verbatim. Write anchors that describe how well a candidate performs the communicative function. The low anchor should describe a candidate that fails the function: it misses the paper's central message, replaces it with generic background, contradicts the paper, or is too brief to be understood. The middle anchor should describe a candidate that achieves the function but with poor selectivity: it either omits a key conceptual element needed to distinguish this paper or drifts into body-level detail, derivations, exhaustive inventories, or citation lists that belong elsewhere. The high anchor should describe a candidate that achieves the function with expert selectivity: it conveys the paper's distinctive message using only the specifics necessary to establish that message, and it deliberately omits material that belongs in the body. A candidate that could stand in the published paper does so because it captures the section's purpose with purposeful brevity, not because it includes every detail. Reward synthesis over inventory. In an abstract, the high candidate states the problem's nature, the approach's key innovation, and the finding's implication without enumerating secondary metrics or equations. In an introduction, the high candidate bridges from motivation into the paper's technical content conceptually, preparing the reader without replacing results with body-level derivations, displayed equations, or exhaustive parameter lists. In a conclusion, the high candidate conveys the paper's main message, key trends, and scope limits in concise synthetic prose, without re-describing methods, result inventories, or figure-by-figure recaps. In related work, the high candidate groups prior work by limitation and motivates the paper's departure, not cataloguing every cited study. Include one criterion for factual consistency with the rest of the paper: a candidate must not contradict, invent, or misattribute results, methods, or claims. Penalize clear contradictions and inventions, not omissions of secondary specifics, conceptual paraphrase, or minor numerical restatements that do not reverse a main conclusion. Include one criterion for fit with the paper's structure and communicative role, including the target word count and hand-offs to adjacent sections. For a conclusion, fit means concise synthesis that closes the paper without introducing new technical content, equations, or reference lists, and hands off cleanly before the references. For an introduction, fit means scope and register that bridge from motivation into the paper's technical content, with a clear transition into the next section—by a brief roadmap or forward pointer, without inventorying every subsequent section—and without body-level derivations, displayed equations, or exhaustive parameter lists. For an abstract, fit means a self-contained summary of appropriate length that moves from problem to findings without detail that belongs in the body. For related work, fit means a focused survey that ends with a clear transition into the paper's contribution.
