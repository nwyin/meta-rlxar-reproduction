Read the whole visible paper. Build a rubric for this missing section in this paper.
A grader will score one candidate at a time, so each criterion must be checkable against
the candidate and the visible paper.

First infer the intended reader and the section's job. Identify the paper's central
contribution, what its readers can reasonably be expected to know, what the surrounding
sections establish, and what this section uniquely needs to contribute. Use the section
type, target length and surrounding text together. An abstract may need to stand alone;
an introduction may need to establish motivation; related work may need to distinguish
prior approaches; a conclusion may need to interpret results or develop an outlook.
Determine which functions matter here rather than requiring every possible function.
Do not require every section to summarize the paper.

Turn those functions into distinct criteria. Require a detail only when its omission
would impair the section's contribution for its intended reader. Distinguish necessary
explanation from background the reader already knows and detail that belongs elsewhere.
Concise writing can still be incomplete; detailed writing can still be proportionate.
Judge what the selection accomplishes, not how much it includes or leaves out.

Keep the criteria concrete. Use the paper's actual claims, methods, findings and terms
to show what meaningful precision looks like. Treat examples as illustrations of a
successful choice, not an inventory every candidate must reproduce. Accept alternative
selections that accomplish the same function. Where a particular fact is essential,
make clear why the reader needs it here. Correct details earn credit for the useful
understanding they add, not merely for appearing in the section.

Write low, middle and high anchors that describe observable differences in how well a
candidate does the section's job. A high anchor should describe precise, sufficient and
proportionate communication that could stand in this paper. A low anchor should describe
a substantive failure, such as obscuring the contribution, contradicting the evidence,
leaving a necessary connection unexplained or burying the main point in peripheral detail.
Explain what makes an omission or addition consequential. Labels such as "good synthesis"
or "coherent narrative" are insufficient without a checkable description. Keep criteria
distinct so no two reward the same thing.
