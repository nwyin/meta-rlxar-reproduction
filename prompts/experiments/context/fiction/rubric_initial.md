You are writing a rubric for grading continuations of one particular story. You have the story so far, which stops mid-scene, the kind of passage required (a continuation), and a target word count. You have no candidate and no reference answer. A grader will later hold one candidate at a time next to the story so far and score it against your rubric. Write it so that any careful grader reaches the same score.

Read the whole story so far closely before you write anything. Note the voice: the narrator's distance from the characters, sentence rhythm, level of diction, the balance of action and dialogue against reflection, how the prose handles time, what it dwells on and what it skips. Note the point of view and tense and whether they ever shift. Note each character who matters: how they speak, what they want, what they know and do not know, what they have just done and how they feel at the moment the text stops. Note the physical facts of the scene in progress: who is present, where, when, what objects are in play. Note what has been set up and not yet paid off: questions raised, threats pending, promises made, tensions building. Then look hard at the exact point where the text stops. What is happening in that moment, and what next beat is the scene moving toward?

From this reading, decide what a strong continuation of this story at this point must do. Turn that judgment into roughly 4 to 8 criteria. Each criterion assesses one distinct function, so a candidate can succeed at one and fail at another. Favor broad functions over narrow checks; do not write a criterion for a single sentence you expect to see.

Make every criterion specific to this story. Name the characters and the scene. Describe the voice in terms a grader can compare against the candidate: not "matches the style" but, for example, "keeps the narrator's dry, understated register and short declarative sentences, and stays inside Mara's head without reporting what others think." A criterion that could be pasted into a rubric for a different story is too generic; rewrite it.

Your criteria must include these two, adapted to this story.

Consistency with the story so far. The candidate must not contradict established facts, character traits, relationships, knowledge, setting, timeline, or the situation at the moment the text stops. List the facts most at risk in this scene: who is in the room, what has just been said, what a character cannot yet know, what has already happened. The high anchor describes a continuation that honors all of these; the low anchor describes one that breaks several, or breaks one the scene turns on.

Fit with the continuation's role and length. The candidate must pick up from exactly where the text stops, in the same scene and moment, without recapping, restarting, retelling what came before, adding a heading, or jumping to another place or time. It must read as the next stretch of the same story and land near the target word count, neither stopping well short nor padding to reach it. Say what picking up correctly looks like here: the sentence or beat the candidate has to follow from.

Choose the remaining criteria from what this story demands. Candidates include: fidelity to the narrative voice and prose style; holding the point of view, tense, and narrative distance; characters acting and speaking as they have been drawn; advancing the scene in progress toward its next beat rather than stalling, circling, or lurching to something unprepared; holding the established tone and genre; using what has been set up; and the quality of the prose itself, with concrete detail, controlled pacing, and sentences that do work. Keep the ones that matter most here and drop the rest. If the story is in a quiet domestic register, a criterion about sustaining dread is wrong; if the scene is an argument, a criterion about how the argument escalates is probably right.

For each criterion, write an id, a description, and low, middle, and high anchors on a 0 to 10 scale. Anchors describe how well a candidate performs that function, and each must name features a grader can check by reading the candidate against the story: what it does, keeps, drops, or contradicts. The three anchors should differ in degree of the same thing. The low anchor describes a continuation that fails this function in a way recognizable for this story. The middle anchor describes partial success: the function is attempted and sometimes achieved, with specific lapses. The high anchor describes excellent performance of this function for this story, in enough detail that a grader can tell excellent from adequate.

Do not use formatting, punctuation, spelling, or typographic conventions as criteria or anchor features. Do not reward or penalize length outside the role and length criterion. Judge only what is on the page against the story so far. Do not write a criterion that requires a particular plot event, since several different next beats could be right; judge instead whether the chosen beat follows from the setup and is handled well.

All criteria carry equal weight and the score is their mean, so make each one important enough to deserve a full share. Keep descriptions and anchors tight. A grader should be able to read the rubric once and apply it.
